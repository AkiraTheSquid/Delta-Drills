## Concept

The app could show you a worked example and ask you to re-read it a few
times. It drills you instead — makes you produce an answer from nothing,
grades it, and only then shows the solution. That choice rests on the
testing effect: the act of retrieving an answer from memory strengthens
that memory more than re-reading the same material does, even when
re-reading feels easier and more comfortable in the moment.

This is why the ladder starts with a lesson — its worked example gives you a
first foothold on a brand-new concept — and then has you write every answer
yourself, with nothing to copy. Struggling to recall, and sometimes getting
it wrong, is not a sign the method is failing: the effort of retrieval is
what strengthens the memory, and an easy re-read skips it.

## Worked example

Imagine two learners studying the same concept for the same total time.
One re-reads the worked example five times. The other reads it once, then
closes it and tries to reproduce the answer from memory five times,
checking after each attempt. A week later, with no further practice, the
second learner recalls the concept better — not because they spent more
time on it, but because every attempt forced an actual retrieval instead of
recognition ("yes, this looks familiar").

This is also why the app's drills change the numbers or framing each time
rather than repeating an identical question: recognizing a memorized
answer is not the same skill as reconstructing it, and only the second one
transfers to a new problem that looks slightly different.
